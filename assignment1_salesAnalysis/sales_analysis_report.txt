1. UNDERSTANDING THE DATA

The dataset records every sale at a coffee chain with three New York City
locations (Astoria, Hell's Kitchen and Lower Manhattan) from January 1 to
June 30, 2023. It has 149,116 rows. Each row is one product line on a
receipt: the date and time, the store, the product (category, type and
detail), the unit price and the quantity. Revenue is not a column, so I
calculated it as quantity x unit price.

Over the six months the chain sold 214,470 items for $698,812 in revenue.
The three stores are almost identical in size: Hell's Kitchen ($236.5K),
Astoria ($232.2K) and Lower Manhattan ($230.1K) are within 3% of each other,
so no single store is carrying or dragging down the business.

Drinks are the business. Coffee (38.6%), tea (28.1%) and drinking chocolate
(10.4%) make up 77% of revenue. Bakery items are the next largest group at
11.8%, and retail goods (coffee beans, loose tea, branded merchandise,
packaged chocolate) add about 10%. Items are cheap: the average drink sells
for about $3.05 and the average pastry for $3.55, so revenue depends on
volume rather than big tickets.

Three patterns stand out:
- Timing: the 7-10 AM window brings in 45.8% of all revenue in just four
  hours. From noon to 5 PM, each hour earns about half of a morning hour
  ($40.8K vs. $80.0K over the six months).
- Growth: monthly revenue more than doubled, from $81.7K in January to
  $166.5K in June (+104%). Only February dipped (-6.8%), which is likely
  because it is a shorter, colder month.
- Concentration: the top 10 product types (led by barista espresso, brewed
  chai, hot chocolate and gourmet brewed coffee) produce 79% of revenue.

For the Python variables I used one real day from the data: Hell's Kitchen
on June 30, 2023, which sold 352 drinks at about $3.10 and 65 pastries at
about $3.61, for $1,325.85. That is well above the $500 benchmark in the
assignment. The average store-day across the whole dataset is about $1,290.

2. RECOMMENDATIONS

Recommendation 1 - Offer a drink + pastry bundle.
The chain sells only 13.2 pastries for every 100 drinks (12.5 at Astoria,
13.0 at Hell's Kitchen, 14.0 at Lower Manhattan). Pastries already sell at a
higher average price than drinks, and nearly every customer is already
buying a drink. A small-discount morning bundle (for example, $1 off a
pastry with any drink) and a "would you like a scone with that?" prompt at
the register are low-cost ways to raise that number. If the attach rate rose
to 20 per 100 drinks, the chain would sell about 12,000 more pastries, worth
roughly $42,800 over six months before the discount.
Data produced: output/rec1_pastry_attach_rate.csv

Recommendation 2 - Use an afternoon promotion to fill the lull.
Revenue drops sharply after 11 AM and stays flat until closing. The stores
are open and staffed in the afternoon but earn about half as much per hour
as in the morning. A 12-5 PM offer, such as a happy-hour price on iced
drinks and tea or a loyalty double-points window, would target spare
capacity rather than competing with the busy morning rush. Morning staffing
should stay focused on speed, since that window drives almost half of sales.
Data produced: output/rec2_revenue_by_hour.csv

Recommendation 3 - Plan staffing and inventory for continued growth,
built around the top sellers.
Revenue doubled in six months at all three stores at the same time, which
suggests demand for the brand is growing rather than one store getting
lucky. Managers should forecast from the recent monthly trend, not from
January, when scheduling staff and ordering supplies. Because 10 product
types make up 79% of revenue, the priority is never running out of espresso
beans, chai, chocolate and the brewed coffees, while slow sellers (for
example green beans, green tea and organic chocolate, each under $1,700 in
six months) are candidates for trimming to simplify the menu and reduce
waste.
Data produced: output/rec3_monthly_revenue.csv, output/rec3_top_products.csv

3. QUESTIONS

1. Does each row represent a full customer order or one line of an order?
   The transaction_id is unique per row, so I cannot tell which drinks and
   pastries were bought together. A true basket ID would make the pastry
   attach rate in Recommendation 1 much more accurate.
2. What are the costs? The data only shows sales prices. Without ingredient,
   labor and rent costs I can recommend how to grow revenue, but not which
   products are most profitable.
3. What caused the growth from March to June: new marketing, price changes,
   seasonality or tourism? Knowing this would show whether the trend is
   likely to continue into the second half of the year.
4. Were there any promotions or discounts during this period? The prices
   look like list prices, so it is unclear whether discounts are already
   reflected in the data.
5. Should the $500 revenue benchmark apply to a single store per day? Every
   store averages well above it, so a higher target might be more useful
   for tracking performance.
